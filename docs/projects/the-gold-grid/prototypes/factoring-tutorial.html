Factoring Step by Step

Factoring is multiplying in reverse. You start with an answer and find what was multiplied to make it.

3 × 4 = 12, so 12 factors into 3 × 4.
2(x + 3) = 2x + 6, so 2x + 6 factors into 2(x + 3).

Why bother? A factored expression is easier to work with. It lets you simplify fractions, solve equations, and see what an expression is built from.

To check any answer: multiply it back out. You should get what you started with.

Each section below has a Why it works box. Read it first. Once you see the multiplying, the factoring is the same thing done backwards.

Three questions to ask

  1. Do all the terms share something? Take it out. (Section 2)
  2. Is it a square minus a square? Use two brackets. (Section 3)
  3. Are there three terms starting with x2? Find two numbers. (Section 4)

1Numbers

Keep dividing by the smallest number that goes in (2, then 3, then 5, …) until you cannot divide any more.

Why it works

Every whole number is built by multiplying smaller building blocks, called primes (2, 3, 5, 7, …). Dividing pulls the blocks out one at a time.

Why start with the smallest? It keeps you organised, so you never miss a block.

Why stop? When nothing smaller divides in, you have reached a single block.

Factor 12

12 ÷ 2 = 612 is even, so divide by 2
6 ÷ 2 = 3divide by 2 again
12 = 2 × 2 × 33 cannot be divided, so stop

Factor 30

30 ÷ 2 = 15divide by 2
15 ÷ 3 = 52 does not go in, so try 3
30 = 2 × 3 × 5

2Take out what is shared

Why it works

A number in front of a bracket multiplies everything inside: 3(2x + 3) means 3 × 2x plus 3 × 3, which is 6x + 9.

Factoring runs that backwards. You spot the 3 hiding in both terms and pull it back out to the front.

Why always try this first? It makes the numbers smaller, and smaller numbers make every later step easier.

  1. Find the biggest number or letter that goes into every term.
  2. Write it in front of a bracket.
  3. Divide each term by it. Put the results inside the bracket.

3 goes into 6 and 9this is what they share
3(2x + 3)6x ÷ 3 = 2x and 9 ÷ 3 = 3
Check: 3 × 2x + 3 × 3 = 6x + 9 ✓multiply back out

4x(x + 2)4x2 ÷ 4x = x and 8x ÷ 4x = 2

3A square minus a square

Use this when there are two terms, both are squares (like x2, 4, 9, 16, 25), and there is a minus between them.

Why it works

Multiply (x − 5)(x + 5) and you get x2 + 5x − 5x − 25.

The +5x and −5x cancel each other, leaving just x2 − 25.

Why one minus and one plus? That is what makes the middle cancel. So when you see a square minus a square with no middle term, you know it came from brackets like these.

  1. Take the square root of each term.
  2. Write two brackets. Put a minus in one and a plus in the other.

Factor x2 − 9

roots: x and 3because 3 × 3 = 9
(x − 3)(x + 3)one minus, one plus
Check: x2 + 3x − 3x − 9 = x2 − 9 ✓the middle cancels

Factor x2 − 49

roots: x and 7because 7 × 7 = 49
(x − 7)(x + 7)

Watch for: this only works with a minus. x2 + 9 cannot be factored, because no pair of brackets makes the middle cancel and still ends in a plus.

4Three terms starting with x2

Why it works

Multiply (x + 2)(x + 3) using a box. Each square is its row times its column.

Add the four squares: x2 + 3x + 2x + 6 = x2 + 5x + 6.

Look where the numbers came from. The middle 5 is 2 + 3. The last 6 is 2 × 3.

That is why you hunt for two numbers that add to the middle and multiply to the end. You are finding the 2 and the 3 that were in the brackets.

  1. Find two numbers that multiply to give the last number and add to give the middle number.
  2. Put one in each bracket: (x    )(x    ).

Factor x2 + 5x + 6

2 × 3 = 6 and 2 + 3 = 5multiply to 6, add to 5
(x + 2)(x + 3)

Factor x2 − 6x + 8

−2 × −4 = 8 and −2 − 4 = −6the middle is minus, so both numbers are minus
(x − 2)(x − 4)minus × minus gives the plus 8
Check: x2 − 4x − 2x + 8 = x2 − 6x + 8 ✓multiply back out

Factor x2 + 3x − 10

5 × −2 = −10 and 5 − 2 = 3the last number is minus, and only plus × minus gives a minus
(x + 5)(x − 2)

Tip: write down the pairs that multiply to the last number, then pick the pair that adds to the middle number. For 6 the pairs are 1 and 6, or 2 and 3.

5Two steps in one question

Sometimes you take out what is shared, and the bracket can then be factored again.

Why this order

2x2 − 18 does not look like a square minus a square. Take out the 2 and the pattern x2 − 9 appears. Taking out what is shared first uncovers the easier problem underneath.

Factor 2x2 − 18

2(x2 − 9)both terms share 2
2(x − 3)(x + 3)a square minus a square
